The Wobble

How a brand-new London bridge was shaken into closure by the people walking across it, and the physics of why.
London Millennium Footbridge · Opened 10 Jun 2000 · Closed 12 Jun 2000 · Reopened 22 Feb 2002
Part I

The
Disaster

A brand-new London bridge, open for just two days before it had to be shut.
The structure

The first new Thames
crossing in a century

A sleek steel suspension footbridge linking St Paul's Cathedral to the brand-new Tate Modern, designed by Foster, Caro and the engineers at Arup. It was meant to be the showpiece of London's millennium celebrations: thin, low and elegant, unlike any bridge before it. That slim, lightweight design is exactly what would make it vulnerable two days later.

It was an instant landmark. But within hours the deck began to move sideways under the crowd, gently at first, then enough that people stopped walking and grabbed the handrails to steady themselves.

The sway begins

It swayed sideways,
and the crowd
swayed with it

The more the deck moved, the more people instinctively adjusted their steps to stay balanced, all leaning the same way at the same time. That synchronised pushing drove the deck even harder, which pulled even more people into step. Each loop made the next one bigger: a runaway feedback loop.

Shut down two days after opening. London nicknamed it the "Wobbly Bridge."
Reopened only after two years and £5 million of repairs.

Engineers judged the swaying too severe to be safe, so they closed it. The bridge had never come close to breaking, it was simply unusable until the cause could be found and fixed.

The key insight

The bridge was
never weak.

It was structurally sound the entire time. The failure was dynamic, not a question of strength. Nobody had designed for the way a moving crowd's footsteps could line up with the deck's natural sideways sway and amplify it. Engineers gave the effect a name:

The phenomenonSynchronous lateral excitation
Part II

The
Physics

Three units, five calculations: resonance, forces, and energy.
WavesSHM

1 · Natural frequency

Every structure has a natural frequency, the rhythm it sways at on its own once disturbed, like a plucked guitar string or a child's swing. For the bridge's central span that rhythm was f₀ = 0.49 Hz (Dallard et al., 2001), so we treat the deck as a mass on a spring:

Angular frequency & period, SHM ω₀ = 2πf₀ = 2π(0.49) = 3.08 rad/s
T = 1 / f₀ = 1 / 0.49 = 2.04 s

In plain terms: left to itself, the bridge naturally sways once about every 2 seconds. That's its built-in rhythm, like a swing's.

Why it resonated

Footsteps in the
danger band

You sway sideways once every two steps, so the lateral push arrives near 1 Hz. The bridge's three spans had lateral modes from ~0.5 to 1.1 Hz, all below the 1.3 Hz threshold, so ordinary walking could drive them (Dallard et al., 2001).

Resonance condition fdrive ≈ f₀ → each push adds to the last → amplitude grows

In plain terms: people's footsteps happened to match the bridge's rhythm, so every step pushed the sway a bit bigger, like timing your pushes on a swing.

SHM

2 · Speed & acceleration

With a central-span sway amplitude A = 70 mm = 0.070 m:

From SHM: v = ωA, a = ω²A vmax = ω₀A = (3.08)(0.070) = 0.216 m/s
amax = ω₀²A = (3.08)²(0.070) = (9.49)(0.070) = 0.66 m/s²
≈ 0.07 g on the central span; stiffer higher modes reached ~0.25 g (Taylor Devices, n.d.)

In plain terms: at full sway the deck was moving sideways about as fast as a slow stroll, enough that people had to grab the railings.

DynamicsEnergy

3 · People as
negative damping

Damping is anything that drains a wobble away: friction, air resistance, shock absorbers. It normally opposes motion. But to stay balanced on a moving deck, walkers pushed with its motion instead, adding energy every cycle rather than removing it. Arup modelled each person as a force that grows with the deck's speed:

Arup pedestrian load model F = cp · v    where cp = 300 N·s/m per person A "dashpot" with the sign flipped, negative damping (Dallard et al., 2001).

In plain terms: instead of calming the bridge down, the crowd was feeding it, pushing the same way it was already moving.

Energy

4 · Energy fed in

Power delivered by one walker is P = Fv = cpv². Averaging over a cycle (⟨v²⟩ = ½vmax²):

Energy added per pedestrian, per cycle ⟨P⟩ = ½cpvmax² = ½(300)(0.216)² = 7.0 W
E = ⟨P⟩·T = (7.0)(2.04) ≈ 14 J per person, per cycle
Positive & repeating → energy accumulates → the runaway.

In plain terms: every step, each person handed the bridge a little energy. Nothing removed it, so it piled up cycle after cycle.

DynamicsEnergy

5 · The tipping point

Instability begins when the crowd's energy input overtakes the structure's damping. Dallard derived a critical number:

Critical crowd, Dallard et al. (2001) Nc = 8π·ζ·fn·M / cp
Arup's walking test, north span (fn = 1.03 Hz):
Nc = 166 people → sudden, violent sway (Newland, 2003)
Check: M·ζ = Nccp/(8πfn) = (166)(300)/(8π·1.03) ≈ 1.9×10³ kg → with ζ ≈ 0.6%, modal mass ≈ 3×10⁵ kg.

In plain terms: below 166 people the bridge stayed calm. Add just a few more, and the sway suddenly exploded.

Crowd synchrony

The crowd locked
into rhythm

Past 166 people, any small sway made walking in time the easier way to balance, so more people synced, feeding in more energy. The same mathematics that describes fireflies flashing together (Strogatz et al., 2005).

Part III

The Fix &
the Fallout

The error, the engineering solution, and the bridge's mark on London.
The design error

An untested
assumption

No part was weak. The mistake was assuming pedestrian loads were vertical and random. Lateral, synchronised crowd loading simply wasn't part of footbridge design in 2000 (Dallard et al., 2001).

Two ways to fix it

Stiffen it, or
damp it

Stiffening would push the natural frequency out of the danger band. But the maths killed that option:

Why stiffening was rejected, f₀ ∝ √(k/m) To raise f₀ from 0.49 Hz → 1.3 Hz: k must rise by (1.3/0.49)² = ≈ 7× A 7× stiffer structure destroys the slim design. → They chose damping.
The retrofit

37 + 52 dampers

Arup fitted 37 viscous (fluid) dampers, like car shock absorbers, plus about 52 tuned mass dampers beneath the deck (Foster + Partners, 2002), raising damping above 20% of critical. The bridge has been stable ever since.

Result Energy now drains faster than any crowd can add it. Reopened Feb 2002.
Impact on society

Two sides

Positive
  • Anchored the Bankside regeneration, linking St Paul's to Tate Modern, which generated ~£100M/yr & ~3,000 jobs for a deprived borough (Tate, 2001).
  • A safe, step-free, car-free Thames crossing now hosting cycle-hire docks.
  • The research permanently improved global footbridge design (Venuti & Bruno, 2009).
Negative
  • The £5M repair pushed cost from a £16M budget to £23.2M of public/charitable funds.
  • Two-year closure denied the public a promised crossing.
  • The "Wobbly Bridge" name stuck, reputational damage to a flagship project.
Impact on the environment

Two sides

Positive
  • Car-free crossing, every walk/cycle trip replaces a vehicle journey, cutting local emissions.
  • Concentrates foot traffic near low-carbon transit (Blackfriars, Southwark).
  • 2025–26 refurbishment reuses the existing aluminium deck panels to cut waste.
Negative
  • Steel & aluminium are energy-intensive, high embodied carbon before anyone crosses (Jena & Kaewunruen, 2021).
  • The retrofit added material shipped from the US & Germany.
  • Construction works in the Thames disrupt the river environment.
The lesson

A sound structure can
still fail dynamically.

The Millennium Bridge held perfectly, but physics it wasn't designed for nearly shook it apart. Resonance, damping and energy aren't abstract: they close bridges.
