The Wobble

How a brand-new London bridge was shaken into closure by the people walking across it, and the physics of why.
London Millennium Footbridge · Opened 10 Jun 2000 · Closed 12 Jun 2000 · Reopened 22 Feb 2002
The disaster

Two days,
then closed

In June 2000 London opened the Millennium Bridge, the first new Thames crossing in over a century, a slim steel suspension footbridge from St Paul's to Tate Modern, engineered by the firm Arup with architects Foster + Partners. About 100,000 people crossed on opening day. Within hours the deck swayed sideways so badly that people grabbed the rails, and it was shut after just two days. It reopened two years later, after £5 million of repairs (about CAD $9.3 million).

320 m span · ~100,000 on day one · open just 2 days · £5M repair
The key insight

The bridge was
never weak

It was structurally sound the whole time. This was a dynamic failureA vibration or motion problem, not a weak-structure problem. The bridge was strong, it just moved.. Nobody had designed for the way a synchronised crowd's footsteps could match the deck's natural sway and amplify it, each cycle feeding the next in a runaway feedback loop. The effect is called:

The phenomenonSynchronous lateral excitationA crowd, walking in step, driving the deck into a sideways vibration.

The mechanism: resonance

The wobble is driven by resonancePushing something at its natural rhythm so the motion keeps growing, like timing pushes on a swing.: push the deck at its own natural rhythm and the motion grows each cycle. The diagram shows the forces on the swaying deck, the crowd's push beats the restoring and damping forces, so the sway keeps building.

Unit 1 · Dynamics

Unit 1: Dynamics

A back-and-forth sway is the projection of circular motion, so we use our circular-motion equations with r = the sway amplitude A = 0.070 m. The span sways at f = 0.49 Hz. (f and A are measured values from Arup's investigation, Dallard et al., 2001; the calculations below are mine.)

Period, frequency & circular motion T = 1/f = 1/0.49 = 2.04 s
vmax = 2πrf = 2π(0.070)(0.49) = 0.22 m/s
amax = 4π²rf² = 4π²(0.070)(0.49)² = 0.66 m/s²

In plain terms: the deck sways every ~2 seconds, moving sideways about as fast as a slow walk.

How I worked it out

  1. Inputs (measured by Arup): f = 0.49 Hz (sways per second) and A = 0.070 m (how far it swings each way).
  2. Period = time for one full sway: T = 1/f = 1/0.49 = 2.04 s.sheet: Period & Frequency
  3. Picture a ball glued to a spinning record, lit from the side so it casts a shadow on the wall. As the ball goes round the circle, its shadow slides straight back and forth, that shadow moves exactly like our swaying bridge. So a sway is just the side-view of circular motion, with circle radius r = the sway amplitude A. That means I'm allowed to use the circle equations from our sheet.
  4. Fastest point is mid-swing: v = 2πrf = 0.22 m/s.sheet: Circular Motion (v = 2πr/T)
  5. Acceleration peaks at the ends of the swing: a = 4π²rf² = 0.66 m/s² (≈ 0.07g).sheet: Centripetal Acceleration
  6. So: one sway every ~2 s, peak speed like a slow walk, enough sideways push to feel and grab the rails.
Unit 2 · Energy & Momentum

Unit 2: Energy & Momentum

Each sideways step gives the deck an impulse (a change in momentum) and does work on it (adds energy). One step: F ≈ 30 N (~4% of body weight), Δt ≈ 0.5 s, distance d = 0.070 m.

Impulse & Work (both on our formula sheet) Momentum: J = FΔt = (30)(0.5) = 15 N·s/step → synced 166 ≈ 2.5×10³ N·s
Energy: W = Fd = (30)(0.070) = 2.1 J/push → ≈ 700 J/cycle (166 people)

In plain terms: random steps cancel, but synced steps add up (momentum) and keep pumping in energy (work) that piles up.

How I worked it out

  1. Estimate one footstep: sideways push F ≈ 30 N (about 4% of a 70 kg person's 690 N weight), contact time Δt ≈ 0.5 s (~2 steps/sec), step distance d = 0.070 m.
  2. Impulse of one step: J = FΔt = 30 × 0.5 = 15 N·s, and that equals the momentum the deck gains (J = Δp).sheet: Impulse, J = FΔt = Δp
  3. Momentum is a vector (it has direction). Out of step, the pushes point random ways and cancel. In step, they all point the same way and add.
  4. Synced crowd: 166 × 15 ≈ 2.5×10³ N·s in one direction every half-sway.sheet: Conservation of Momentum
  5. Energy each push adds: W = Fd = 30 × 0.070 = 2.1 J.sheet: Work, W = Fd
  6. ~2 pushes per cycle ≈ 4 J each, × 166 people ≈ 700 J added every cycle. They push with the motion (like pumping a swing), so it's positive energy added every cycle. Nothing drains it, so it piles up as the deck's kinetic energy (½mv²).sheet: Kinetic Energy
Beyond our course (research)

Why it didn't stop

Every walker pushes a little with the sway, so the crowd acts as negative dampingDamping normally drains a wobble away. Here it was reversed, the crowd fed energy in instead of removing it., it feeds energy in instead of draining it. There is a tipping-point crowd size, the critical number, where the bridge can no longer damp the wobble away faster than the crowd pumps it up (Dallard et al., 2001).

Critical crowd (Dallard et al., 2001) Nc = 8π·ζ·f·M / cp  →  166 people (north span) 8π = a fixed number from the math (≈ 25) · ζ (zeta) = how much the bridge naturally damps itself · f = sway frequency · M = moving mass of the deck · cp = how hard one person pushes

In plain terms: below the critical number it stays calm; above it, the sway runs away, and the bigger it gets, the more people lock into step (synchronisation, the same maths as fireflies flashing together, Strogatz et al., 2005).

What this means

  1. Think of a tug-of-war over the sway: the crowd adds energy (negative damping), the bridge's own damping removes it.
  2. The formula just compares those two. Nc is the crowd size where they exactly balance, the tipping point.
  3. Below Nc: the bridge wins, the wobble dies out → calm.
  4. Above Nc: the crowd wins, energy piles up faster than it drains → runaway sway.
  5. In Arup's walking test on the north span, that tipping point was 166 people, one more and it suddenly went unstable.
  6. It snowballs: a bigger sway makes it even easier to walk in step, so more people sync and push harder.

The error & the fix

Assumption,
then dampers

The mistake wasn't a weak part, it was assuming foot traffic was vertical and random. Stiffening the bridge was rejected (you'd need ~7× the stiffness to move it out of the danger band, which wrecks the slim design), so they added damping: 37 viscous dampers plus ~52 tuned mass dampers (Foster + Partners, 2002).

Why not stiffen? (f ∝ √(k/m)) (1.3 / 0.49)² ≈ 7× stiffer needed → impractical → damping chosen

In plain terms: the dampers drain energy faster than any crowd can add it.

Impact on society

Two sides

Positive
  • Anchored the Bankside regeneration, linking St Paul's to Tate Modern, which generated ~£100M/yr & ~3,000 jobs for a deprived borough (Tate, 2001).
  • A safe, step-free, car-free Thames crossing, now hosting cycle-hire docks.
  • The research permanently improved global footbridge design (Venuti & Bruno, 2009).
Negative
  • The £5M repair pushed cost from a £16M budget to £23.2M of public/charitable funds.
  • Two-year closure denied the public a promised crossing.
  • The "Wobbly Bridge" name stuck, reputational damage to a flagship project.
Impact on the environment

Two sides

Positive
  • Car-free crossing, every walk or cycle trip replaces a vehicle journey, cutting local emissions.
  • Concentrates foot traffic near low-carbon transit (Blackfriars, Southwark).
  • The 2025–26 refurbishment reuses the existing aluminium deck panels to cut waste.
Negative
  • Steel & aluminium are energy-intensive, high embodied carbon before anyone crosses (Jena & Kaewunruen, 2021).
  • The retrofit added material shipped from the US & Germany.
  • Construction works in the Thames disrupt the river environment.
The lesson

A sound structure can
still fail dynamically

The bridge held perfectly, but physics it wasn't designed for nearly shook it apart. Forces, momentum and energy aren't abstract: an ordinary crowd nearly closed a perfectly strong bridge, and the fix was simply to drain the energy back out.
