The Millennium Wobble Bridge

How a brand new London bridge wobbled into closure by the people walking across it, and the physics of why.
London Millennium Footbridge · Opened 10 Jun 2000 · Closed 12 Jun 2000 · Reopened 22 Feb 2002
The disaster

Two days,
then closed

In June 2000 London opened the Millennium Bridge, the first new Thames crossing in over a century, a slim steel suspension footbridge from St Paul's to Tate Modern, engineered by the firm Arup with architects Foster + Partners. About 100,000 people crossed on opening day. Within hours the deck swayed sideways so badly that people grabbed the rails, and it was shut after just two days. It reopened two years later, after £5 million of repairs (about CAD $9.3 million).

320 m span · ~100,000 on day one · open just 2 days · £5M repair
Facts: Dallard et al., 2001; Tate, 2001
The key insight

The bridge was
never weak

It was structurally sound the whole time. This was a dynamic failureA vibration or motion problem, not a weak structure problem. The bridge was strong, it just moved.. Nobody had designed for the way a synchronised crowd's footsteps could match the deck's natural sway and amplify it, each cycle feeding the next in a runaway feedback loop. The effect is called:

The phenomenonSynchronous lateral excitationA crowd, walking in step, driving the deck into a sideways vibration.
Beyond our course

The mechanism: resonance

The wobble is driven by resonancePushing something at its natural rhythm so it keeps growing, like timing your pushes on a swing.: push the deck at the right rhythm and it swings bigger every time. The diagram below shows the forces. The crowd pushes the same way the deck is already moving, so it adds energy instead of fighting it, and the sway gets bigger.

Unit 1 · Dynamics

Unit 1: Dynamics

A back and forth sway is the shadow of circular motion, so we use our circular motion equations with r = the sway amplitude A = 0.070 m. The deck is fastest in the middle of the swing and stops at each end to turn around (just like a swing), so the results below are the maximum speed and acceleration. The bridge sways at f = 0.49 Hz. (f and A are measured by Arup, Dallard et al., 2001; the calculations below are mine.)

Period, frequency & circular motion T = 1/f = 1/0.49 = 2.04 s
vmax = 2πrf = 2π(0.070)(0.49) = 0.22 m/s
amax = 4π²rf² = 4π²(0.070)(0.49)² = 0.66 m/s²

In plain terms: the deck sways every ~2 seconds, moving sideways about as fast as a slow walk.

How I worked it out

  1. Inputs (measured by Arup): f = 0.49 Hz (sways per second) and A = 0.070 m (how far it swings each way).
  2. Period = time for one full sway: T = 1/f = 1/0.49 = 2.04 s.
  3. Picture a ball glued to a spinning record, lit from the side so it casts a shadow on the wall. As the ball goes round, its shadow slides straight back and forth, exactly like our swaying bridge. So a sway is just the side view of circular motion, with circle radius r = the sway amplitude A.
  4. Fastest point is mid swing: v = 2πrf = 0.22 m/s.
  5. Acceleration peaks at the ends of the swing: a = 4π²rf² = 0.66 m/s². That's small, about 0.07g (0.66 ÷ 9.8 ≈ 0.07).
  6. So: one sway every ~2 s, peak speed like a slow walk, enough sideways push to feel and grab the rails.
Unit 2 · Energy & Momentum

Unit 2: Energy & Momentum

Each sideways step gives the deck an impulse (a change in momentum) and does work on it (adds energy). As you walk you rock slightly side to side, so each step pushes sideways with about 5% of your body weight, roughly F ≈ 30 N for a 60 kg person. Δt ≈ 0.5 s, distance d = 0.070 m. (d = 0.070 m and 166 are measured by Arup, Dallard et al., 2001; F and Δt are standard estimates; the rest are my calculations.)

Impulse & Work Momentum: J = FΔt = (30)(0.5) = 15 N·s/step → synced 166 ≈ 2.5×10³ N·s
Energy: W = Fd = (30)(0.070) = 2.1 J/push → ≈ 700 J/cycle (166 people)

In plain terms: random steps cancel, but synced steps add up (momentum) and keep pumping in energy (work) that piles up.

How I worked it out

  1. Estimate one footstep: sideways push F ≈ 30 N. A person's weight is Fg = mg = 60 × 9.8 = 590 N, and the sideways push is about 5% of that: 0.05 × 590 = ≈ 30 N. People walk about 2 steps per second, so each step lasts Δt = 1 ÷ 2 ≈ 0.5 s. Step distance d = 0.070 m.
  2. Impulse of one step: J = FΔt = 30 × 0.5 = 15 N·s. This equals the momentum the deck gains, J = Δp.
  3. Momentum is a vector (it has direction). Out of step, the pushes point random ways and cancel. In step, they all point the same way and add.
  4. Synced crowd: 166 × 15 ≈ 2.5×10³ N·s in one direction every half sway.
  5. Energy each push adds: W = Fd = 30 × 0.070 = 2.1 J.
  6. ~2 pushes per cycle ≈ 4 J each, × 166 people ≈ 700 J added every cycle. They push with the motion (like pumping a swing), so it's positive energy added every cycle. Nothing drains it, so it piles up as the deck's kinetic energy (½mv²).
Beyond our course (research)

Why it didn't stop

Every walker pushes a little with the sway, so the crowd acts as negative dampingDamping normally drains a wobble away. Here it was reversed, the crowd fed energy in instead of removing it., it feeds energy in instead of draining it. There is a tipping point crowd size, the critical number, where the bridge can no longer damp the wobble away faster than the crowd pumps it up (Dallard et al., 2001).

Critical crowd (Dallard et al., 2001) Nc = 8π·ζ·f·M / cp  →  166 people (north span) 8π = a fixed number from the math (≈ 25) · ζ (zeta) = how much the bridge naturally damps itself · f = sway frequency · M = moving mass of the deck · cp = how hard one person pushes
Arup measured ζ, M and cp and got Nc ≈ 166, I'm citing their result, not deriving it (I don't have those values).

In plain terms: below the critical number it stays calm; above it, the sway runs away, and the bigger it gets, the more people lock into step (synchronisation, the same maths as fireflies flashing together, Strogatz et al., 2005).

What this means

  1. Nc = the critical number: the tipping point crowd size where the bridge can no longer calm itself. For this bridge, 166.
  2. Think of a tug of war over the sway: the crowd adds energy (negative damping), the bridge's own damping removes it.
  3. The formula compares the two: the bridge's defence (damping ζ, mass M, frequency f) on top, the per person push (cp) on the bottom. They exactly balance at Nc.
  4. Below Nc: the bridge wins, the wobble dies out → calm.
  5. Above Nc: the crowd wins, energy piles up faster than it drains → runaway sway.
  6. In Arup's walking test on the north span, that tipping point was 166 people, one more and it suddenly went unstable.

The science behind it

  1. Each walker pushes the deck a little sideways each step.
  2. Random steps point different ways and cancel out.
  3. Near 0.49 Hz the walkers fall into step, so their pushes line up and add (synchronisation).
  4. That synced crowd feeds energy into the sway, like a swing pushed in time, while the bridge's damping drains it out. A tug of war.
  5. Below 166 the damping wins (calm); above 166 the crowd wins (runaway).
  6. Retrofit = the dampers draining energy so fast the crowd can't win. (Teaching model tuned to 0.49 Hz and 166, not an exact engineering sim.)

The math behind it

What the model computes

  1. closeness = how near the step rate is to 0.49 Hz (1 = right on it, 0 = far off).
  2. in-tune people = people × closeness (only people near the rhythm count).
  3. grow = (in-tune people) − 166.
  4. Each moment: new sway = old sway + (old sway × grow × a tiny time step). Positive grow → sway gets bigger; negative → it shrinks.
  5. The deck is drawn as sway × sin(time), so it rocks side to side.
  6. Retrofit makes the 166 huge, so grow is always negative and the wobble dies.

The real physics it stands for (beyond our course)

  1. The deck is a driven, damped oscillator: m·a = −kx − cv + F. That's Newton's 2nd law (F = ma) with a spring-like pull back to centre (−kx) and a damping force (−cv).
  2. The crowd's push acts like negative damping: F = cp·v, it pushes with the motion, adding energy instead of removing it.
  3. It runs away once the crowd beats the bridge's damping, at the critical number Nc = 8π·ζ·f·M / cp ≈ 166.
  4. My model is a simple stand-in for that same balance, tuned to the real 0.49 Hz and 166.
The error & the fix

Assumption,
then dampers

No part was weak. The mistake: they thought people only push down when they walk, and forgot people also push a little sideways. That little sideways push is what shook the bridge. To fix it, making the bridge stiffer would take about 7 times more stiffness and wreck the slim design, so they added dampers instead: 37 viscous dampers plus ~52 tuned mass dampers (Foster + Partners, 2002).

Why not stiffen? (natural frequency f = (1/2π)√(k/m)) With mass fixed, f ∝ √k, so to change f you scale k by the ratio squared:
k₂/k₁ = (f₂/f₁)² = (1.3 / 0.49)² ≈ 7× stiffer needed → impractical → damping chosen 0.49 Hz is Arup's measured value (Dallard et al., 2001); 1.3 Hz is the target needed to move it out of the danger band.

In plain terms: the dampers drain energy faster than any crowd can add it.

Impact on society

Two sides

Positive
  • Anchored the Bankside regeneration, linking St Paul's to Tate Modern, which generated ~£100M/yr & ~3,000 jobs for a deprived borough (Tate, 2001).
  • A safe, step free, car free Thames crossing, now hosting cycle hire docks.
  • The research permanently improved global footbridge design (Venuti & Bruno, 2009).
Negative
  • The £5M repair pushed cost from a £16M budget to £23.2M of public/charitable funds.
  • Two year closure denied the public a promised crossing.
  • The "Wobbly Bridge" name stuck, reputational damage to a flagship project.
Impact on the environment

Two sides

Positive
  • Car free crossing, every walk or cycle trip replaces a vehicle journey, cutting local emissions.
  • Concentrates foot traffic near low carbon transit (Blackfriars, Southwark).
  • The 2025–26 refurbishment reuses the existing aluminium deck panels to cut waste.
Negative
  • Steel & aluminium are energy intensive, high embodied carbon before anyone crosses (Jena & Kaewunruen, 2021).
  • The retrofit added material shipped from the US & Germany.
  • Construction works in the Thames disrupt the river environment.
The lesson

A sound structure can
still fail dynamically

The bridge held perfectly, but physics it wasn't designed for nearly shook it apart. Forces, momentum and energy aren't abstract: an ordinary crowd nearly closed a perfectly strong bridge, and the fix was simply to drain the energy back out.
